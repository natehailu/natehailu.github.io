Disruptions don't get worse at night. Recovery does.

2,431,756 flights, twelve months, and one metric that the published on-time data does not contain: how many ways out of a disruption a passenger actually has left.

Role
Sole analyst
Timeline
September 2026
Data
BTS On-Time Performance, Jun 2025 – May 2026
Tools

The question came from the counter, not the data

I work airline operations. When a flight cancels, I am one of the people a hundred passengers walk toward. After enough of those nights, a pattern became obvious that I could not find anywhere in the published statistics.

A cancellation at nine in the morning is an inconvenience. The same cancellation at nine at night is a hotel voucher, a missed wedding, a passenger who will not make their connection tomorrow either. Same aircraft, same reason, same row in a database. Completely different night for the person in front of me.

Every published measure of airline reliability treats those two events identically. On-time rate, cancellation rate, average delay minutes, none of them can tell the difference. So I went looking for a measure that could.

The data

The Bureau of Transportation Statistics publishes a row for every domestic flight operated by a reporting carrier: schedule, actual times, delay minutes attributed to five causes, cancellations, diversions, and the tail number of the aircraft.

I pulled twelve months, June 2025 through May 2026, and scoped it to flights touching a United hub or Lubbock, which is where I work. Every carrier was kept rather than filtering to one, because a single airline's numbers mean nothing without the industry beside them.

Pipeline

  • Twelve monthly archives downloaded and unpacked, roughly 30MB compressed each.
  • Cut from 100+ columns to the 33 the question needs, which took the working set from gigabytes to something that fits in memory.
  • Scoped by origin or destination to eight airports, retaining 2,431,756 of 7,047,314 flights.
  • Derived fields computed per row, then the whole set combined for the analysis.
  • Resumable and reproducible end to end. Re-running it produces the same file.

A deliberate choice

I defined disrupted as cancelled, diverted, or arriving 45 or more minutes late, rather than using the industry's 15-minute on-time standard.

Fifteen minutes measures whether a flight was late. Forty-five measures whether a connection broke. This study is about what happens to the passenger afterward, so the threshold had to match the consequence rather than the convention.

The metric

For every flight in the dataset, I counted the later departures that day on the same route, on any carrier, leaving at least sixty minutes after the original. Sixty because a passenger has to be told, reissued, and physically get to another gate.

If a disrupted flight had zero of those, the passenger was not rebooked that day. They were stranded.

That number is the thing the published statistics cannot see. It is not a measure of how often airlines fail. It is a measure of how much room is left to recover when they do, and it turns out to behave completely differently.

Computing it across 2.4 million rows naively would have taken hours. Sorting every day-and-route group into one global array and using a single binary search brought it to ten seconds. I verified the fast version against a slow, obviously correct implementation, row for row, before trusting any of the numbers below.

What the data showed

Disruption risk roughly triples between dawn and dusk. That part was expected. What was not expected is how much faster the ways out disappear.

A passenger disrupted in the evening is stranded 6.7 times more often than one disrupted in the morning. 58.2 percent against 8.7 percent.

The mechanism is in the first chart. Average remaining options fall from 8.5 in the early morning to 0.36 by eight at night and to zero by eleven. Disruption risk triples across the day. Recovery capacity falls by ninety-five percent.

Which means the outcome is effectively decided at the moment the flight is cancelled. Everything after that is the passenger finding out slowly, in a line.

Three findings I did not go looking for

Regional carriers strand at twice the mainline rate

SkyWest 6.10 percent, Republic 6.22, Envoy 6.17, PSA 8.79, against United 3.12, Delta 2.35, Alaska 2.02. The striking part is that this does not track reliability. American disrupts more often than SkyWest, 16.8 percent against 15.1, and strands less, 4.07 against 6.10.

Frequency drives recovery, not reliability. A route with twelve daily departures absorbs a failure. A route with two does not. That makes it a network design finding rather than an operational one.

Weather is the rarest primary cause in the set and by far the most damaging. It produces delays half again as long as anything else and strands more than twice as often as air traffic constraints. Weather disruptions are longer, and long disruptions push into the part of the day where nothing is left.

My own station is in the ten worst

Lubbock strands 6.09 percent of its flights, putting it among the ten worst airports in the dataset above five thousand annual departures. A small station with few daily departures has almost no slack, which is the same finding as the regional carriers, seen from the ground instead of the fleet.

What this does not measure

Options are counted on the same origin and destination pair only. Real reaccommodation can route a passenger through a different connection, onto a partner carrier, or to a nearby airport with ground transport.

So this is a floor on available recovery, not literal stranding. The true figure is lower than 31.6 percent, and it is lower by more at the big multi-airport hubs than at places like Lubbock.

I state it this way because a reviewer who works in the industry will see the gap in about ten seconds, and a number presented without its limits is worth less than a smaller number presented with them. Closing that gap by layering in connecting itineraries is the obvious second version of this analysis.

What I would do with it

The finding points somewhere specific. If the outcome is already decided when the cancellation is entered, then speed at the counter is the wrong lever. Nobody should be standing at that counter in the first place.

  • Reaccommodate before the announcement. The system knows the passenger list, the remaining capacity, and, with this metric, whether any capacity exists at all. Assign and push before a queue can form.
  • Use the metric as a trigger, not a report. Zero remaining options is a condition a system can detect in advance and act on, not a statistic to review next quarter.
  • Reposition aircraft against the rotation, not against the schedule. Where a recovery flight is worth running, use the aircraft whose next assignment already begins at that station, so the repositioning was going to happen anyway.

Each of these had to survive real operational objections before I trusted it, not after. I ran every proposal through a working airline person's first ten questions and kept only what held.

  • "There's no spare aircraft to send." True at the station, not true within reach of it. A reserve crew can deadhead a spare in from a nearby hub empty and fly it back full, which returns everyone to their own base for legal rest. The break-even is a number, not an opinion: send the aircraft when stranded passengers exceed ferry cost divided by avoided cost per passenger, a rule an operations controller can apply at 9pm without asking permission.
  • "Calling in a later flight only works for mechanical delays." Airlines already fly unscheduled extra sections during irregular operations, so adding a flight tonight is a real tool, not a fantasy. The binding constraints are gates, slots, crew legality, and whether a candidate aircraft actually exists, which is answerable directly from the tail numbers already in the dataset.
  • "A faster interface won't clear the line." It won't. Two hundred people arriving at a podium inside ten minutes is an arrival-rate problem, not a service-rate problem. The fix that survives is assigning and pushing the best available option before anyone queues, so most passengers never need the counter at all.

The constraint I held throughout every one of these: nothing that asks an airline to spend money against its own interest. Every surviving fix uses capacity, crew time, or labor the airline was already paying for, just spent earlier or spent better.

One case still doesn't have an honest fix: a late cancellation at a thin station with no aircraft positioned nearby and no alternate airport within reach. I sized it rather than talk around it, and said plainly what's still possible there, an early warning and a confirmed seat for tomorrow, and what isn't, getting them home tonight.

What I took from it

The most useful thing I brought to this was not a technique. It was knowing which question to ask. The 45-minute threshold, the 60-minute rebooking buffer, and the entire idea of counting remaining options came from standing at a service desk, not from the dataset.

The second thing was learning to say what a number does not cover. The honest version of this analysis is less impressive than the version I could have written, and considerably more defensible.

The third thing was learning to defend a recommendation rather than just make one. Proposing "add capacity" or "redesign the interface" takes an afternoon. Pressure-testing each one against a skeptical operator, and rebuilding the two that broke into something that actually survived, took longer than the original analysis. That process is what I'd point a reviewer toward if they want to see how I think, more than the findings themselves.
